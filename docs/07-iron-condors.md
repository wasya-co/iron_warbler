
I just think iron condors are a bad idea. Noone talks about it in into derivatives classes, but I will.

An iron condor is two credit spreads. Let's say XYZ is trading at $100. Let's say you are bearish on it. You sell a $120 call and buy a $130 call. So far so good. But then, you may enter an iron condor: you may sell an $80 put, and buy a $70 put. The put spread is "free" in that since it is opposite of the call spread, it uses the same liquidity. If things go well, some may argue that such a position doubles your profit. But notice the "if" conditional. And don't for a moment believe that markets give anything for free. If you double the possible profit, you double the risk. Which means it's not free at all. But is an iron condor a good idea?

Over years of practice I've decided that iron condors are not a good idea, and I only enter them then I panic, and even then I often regret it.

(I also regret not entering iron condors, as I regret missed opportunities *if* the trade would have gone my way - but that's a different matter.)

Myself, I'm slowly learning to keep cash as a position, but lets assume I have a continuous position in XYZ. Let's see why iron condors are a bad idea. When volatility rises, all option prices rise, and both legs lose money. If the stock moves to $80, in-line with your bearish sentiment, you're not going to be able to close your 70-80 credit put spread without losing money. And the other leg, the 120-130 credit call spread, may actually also be losing money at the same time, due to increased volatility. You're stuck, you can't get out of this position, you can roll both legs but then that goes against your bearish sentiment.

If it wasn't an iron condor, if it was just the 120-130 credit call spread, you would have rolled it forward 1 week (or however you do it - I play weeklies, fridays, so I roll one week forward). You would have made money on the roll-over (heightened volatility of "this" spread means heightened volatility of "next" spread, so on net balance you gain by rolling). And you are still in line with your beliefs: you are bearish on the stock, and you have a bearish position.

If you have an iron condor, you can roll both legs, and make money on rolling both, but you will be going contra your own belief/strategy. With an iron condor you are neutral, not bearish. Of course, time and again, the market will then swing wildly, and put you at maximum loss on one of the legs. And then you have to either take a loss - max loss at that - or keep rolling the position that you don't believe in. Not to mention you need liquidity to roll it, in this case, $1000. And you can't close one leg, even at a loss, if you don't have liquidity.

Maybe my explanation is not the best, but time and again, I have seen myself regretting entering iron condors, and so I don't recommend them, or only recommend them as an extreme strategy. Yes, without iron condors you are leaving money on the table, but the cost (risk) equation is such that you actually want that, you want to leave money on the table. It's called discipline.

And yes, it is somewhat unusual to describe discipline as leaving money on the table. But remember: you only make a profit *if* the trade goes your way. That *if* is what makes the restrained discipline.
