

I call it reckless to remind myself what it is. Basically, if a credit spread is under water or deep under water, I'll add the second leg to make it an iron condor. Sometimes it works, and sometimes it really, really doesn't. This reckless defence part, the second leg, goes against my strategy and my belief - whatever it was. If my strategy resulted in me holding a spread significantly under water, I may enter reckless defence, but I should not change my strategy only because of this. The defence is opposite of the strategy. Make sure that the defence does not become the strategy, since they are opposites and would conflict.

How would a defence become a strategy, you ask? Let me illustrate. Let's say XYZ is trading at $100, you are bullish, and entered a $70-80 credit put spread. Okay, now let's imagine XYZ dumps to $70. That's max loss for you in this case. What you can do is enter a $80-90 credit call spread. Or less extreme, let's say you enter a $100-110 credit call spread. You're losing less money on the position. But of course, then XYZ pops up right back to $100 or $105. What do you do? You can close your $70-80 credit put spread, for the maximum profit. But then if you (1) roll $100-110 call spread forward, and even if you (2) add $70-80 credit put spread back, you are running against your own strategy. You are either (1) bearish or (2) neutral whereas you were originally bullish. The defence, the $100-110 credit call spread, has become the main leg in the position, and you don't want it, you want to get rid of it, it's losing money, but you don't want to take the loss, and it goes from bad to worse - or maybe it gets better - and maybe you exit well or maybe you exit terribly. The point is, a defensive spread should not become your main spread just because you are holding it. It run opposite of your strategy.
